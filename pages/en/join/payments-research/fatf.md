Opening an account at a crypto exchange in Taiwan means uploading ID, and sending virtual assets out means filling in the recipient's details. Opening a bank account means explaining where the money comes from. Most of these rules trace back to one international body, the Financial Action Task Force (FATF). The FATF cannot legislate and its recommendations are not a treaty, yet most countries base their anti-money laundering laws on them. Its public lists can also move a country's capital flows, so research on anonymous payments has to deal with FATF rules.

## Origins and members

The FATF was set up at the 1989 G7 summit in Paris to develop measures against money laundering. Its secretariat sits within the OECD in Paris. It has 40 members: 38 jurisdictions plus two regional organisations, the European Commission and the Gulf Co-operation Council. Russia's membership has been suspended since 24 February 2023. Most members are G20 or European countries; the Asia-Pacific members are China, Hong Kong, India, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, Malaysia and Singapore.

With only 40 members, the FATF extends its standards to the whole world through nine FATF-style regional bodies (FSRBs). Together they form the Global Network, which covers more than 200 jurisdictions. Taiwan takes part in the Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering (APG) as Chinese Taipei, and the APG carries out its mutual evaluations; Taiwan has not joined the FATF itself.

## The 40 Recommendations and their expansion

The FATF is built around its 40 Recommendations: countries write them into their own laws, and the FATF checks how well they are applied. The scope has widened over time:

- 1990: the first 40 Recommendations, aimed at money laundering
- 2001: after 9/11, special recommendations on terrorist financing
- 2012: the special recommendations folded into today's 40, with proliferation financing added
- 2018–2019: Recommendation 15 amended to cover virtual assets and VASPs
- 2023: Recommendation 8 amended in response to criticism that non-profits were being over-regulated

Each expansion brought more businesses and transactions under identity checks and reporting. Virtual assets are the latest, and the most relevant to research on anonymous payments.

## Working methods

### Mutual evaluations

Every country is evaluated periodically by a team of experts from other countries. The ratings come in two parts:

- **Technical compliance**: each of the 40 Recommendations is rated compliant, largely compliant, partially compliant or non-compliant, to show whether the laws are in place
- **Effectiveness**: each of 11 immediate outcomes is rated high, substantial, moderate or low, to show whether the system actually works

Depending on the results, a country is placed in regular or enhanced follow-up and reports its progress at set intervals.

### The grey list and the black list

The FATF plenary meets every February, June and October and decides two public lists. The grey list, formally "jurisdictions under increased monitoring", covers countries with strategic deficiencies that have committed to an action plan with the FATF. Listed countries must fix the deficiencies by a deadline and leave the list after passing an on-site visit. The black list, formally "high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action", asks all countries to apply enhanced scrutiny to the jurisdictions on it, and countermeasures in the most serious cases. In October 2026 the black list was North Korea, Iran and Myanmar: countermeasures for North Korea and Iran, enhanced due diligence for Myanmar.

The grey list had 9 jurisdictions in February 2018, peaked at 26 in June 2023 and stood at 18 in October 2026. Reviews were paused in 2020 because of COVID-19, so the June list repeated February's. In October 2022 Myanmar moved from the grey list to the black list, the only escalation in the period.

## Sources of leverage

FATF recommendations carry no penalties; their force comes from how governments and the financial industry respond. Regulators pass the lists on to financial institutions: the US Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) usually issues a notice after each plenary drawing attention to the listed jurisdictions. Taiwan's Investigation Bureau republishes the FATF statements on its website. In June 2025, for example, the Financial Supervisory Commission forwarded the Bureau's letter reminding financial institutions to apply enhanced scrutiny or proportionate countermeasures to black-listed jurisdictions. Banks also build the lists into their own risk assessments, and correspondent banks may scale back or end business with listed countries.

Two International Monetary Fund (IMF) researchers, Kida and Paetzold, estimated the combined effect in a 2021 working paper. Using data on 89 emerging and developing economies from 2000 to 2017, they estimated that capital inflows fell by about 7.6% of GDP on average after grey-listing. Foreign direct investment, portfolio investment and other investment all declined.

<!-- fatf-imf -->

The authors note that grey-listing events are only about 0.7% of the sample. The reasons for the drop (banks de-risking, investors using the list as a risk signal) are inferred rather than tested. The decline is about the size of the 25th percentile of quarterly declines in emerging and developing economies during the global financial crisis.

## Taiwan's position

The APG adopted Taiwan's third-round mutual evaluation report in 2019, after an on-site visit in November 2018. Seven of the 11 effectiveness outcomes were rated substantial and four moderate (supervision, preventive measures, transparency of legal persons, and money laundering investigation and prosecution). On technical compliance, 10 Recommendations were compliant, 26 largely compliant and 4 partially compliant, with none non-compliant. According to Taiwan's Ministry of Justice, this placed Taiwan in regular follow-up, a level few APG members have reached.

Among the 199 jurisdictions with fourth-round evaluations, only six have more outcomes rated substantial or high than Taiwan. All six are FATF members (France, the United States, the United Kingdom, Spain, Italy and Israel). Taiwan shares the top score among non-members with Kazakhstan, Jersey and Bermuda.

The reports came out between 2014 and 2024, under different standards and with different assessment teams, which limits cross-country comparison.

## Assessments of the FATF

### Effectiveness

In the FATF's own evaluations, 38.5% of all effectiveness ratings across 199 jurisdictions are low, and 41.7% of jurisdictions have no outcome rated substantial or better (the leftmost bar in the chart above).

Critics often cite Ronald Pol (2020). He estimates that about $3 trillion in criminal funds is generated each year and authorities confiscate about $3 billion, a success rate of about 0.1%. Using an estimate from the data company LexisNexis, compliance costs come to about $304 billion, more than a hundred times what is recovered. Pol notes that the data are poorly validated and the methods inconsistent, so the findings cannot be definitive. He adds that whether the real success rate is 0.1% or ten times that, the gap between policy intent and results is large.

From the supporting side, Julia Morse (2019) describes "market enforcement": the FATF has no sanctions of its own, so markets punish countries that fall short. In her study, listed countries saw cross-border bank liabilities fall, which gave governments a reason to reform. According to a commentary from the Royal United Services Institute (RUSI), a UK think tank, the FATF has helped African countries break a cycle of repeated grey-listing; South Africa, Nigeria, Mozambique and Burkina Faso all left the list in October 2025. Case-Ruchala and Nance (2020) re-ran Morse's analysis and found that her sample had only 10 countries. Adding every country listed in the same period made the effect disappear.

### Fairness

Forty members set the rules by consensus, and the rules apply to more than 200 jurisdictions. According to articles from RUSI and the U4 Anti-Corruption Resource Centre, African countries have almost no seat at the FATF table yet must carry out its decisions. Some commentators also see the lists as a geopolitical tool.

Sorting grey-listed jurisdictions by World Bank income group tests the claim that the lists target developing countries.

<!-- fatf-share -->

Between 67% and 95% of grey-listed jurisdictions were not high-income, against 59% to 64% for the world as a whole, so the list does lean towards poorer countries. Every plenary also had one to six high-income economies on the list, such as Panama, the Cayman Islands, Malta, the UAE, Monaco and Bulgaria. According to a Basel Institute on Governance article, the grey list names countries working with the FATF to fix deficiencies.

Evaluation results differ between members and non-members too. FATF members average 4.7 outcomes rated substantial or high; non-members average 1.4. Among high-income economies alone, members average 5.3 and non-members 2.0.

<!-- fatf-groups -->

Critics can read the gap as peer review going easy on members; supporters can read it as members putting more resources into their systems. These data reveal a correlation only, so the two readings cannot be told apart. Regional members are not uniform either. The Gulf Co-operation Council is an FATF member, and Saudi Arabia is a member in its own right. Within the same bloc, the UAE was grey-listed from March 2022 to February 2024, and Kuwait was added in February 2026 and remains on the list.

### The cost and who bears it

To reduce risk, banks may simply close accounts or refuse transfers, a practice known as de-risking. Non-profits, humanitarian remittances and people already on the edge of the banking system bear the brunt. Recommendation 8 used to treat the whole non-profit sector as vulnerable to terrorist financing. One example in a RUSI commentary is Nicaragua, where the government used compliance with Recommendation 8 as cover to crack down on civil society.

The FATF launched a project on unintended consequences in 2021 and amended Recommendation 8 in November 2023. Since then, countries are expected to identify the non-profits actually at risk instead of applying measures to the whole sector. The Global NPO Coalition on FATF issued a statement welcoming the change as a response to long-standing demands, and the coalition will watch how it plays out in the new round of evaluations. Civil society, by taking part, helped bring the revision about. According to a RUSI commentary, the revision may be cosmetic. According to the FATF's own stocktake, de-risking is hard to attribute directly to its standards, with supervisory pressure and reputational concerns also playing a part.

### Privacy implications

The FATF requires financial institutions to know their customers, keep records and report suspicious transactions. Wire transfers must carry information on the sender and the recipient, a requirement known as the Travel Rule (Recommendation 16). These requirements now reach virtual assets. In 2021 Coin Center, a US crypto policy group, submitted comments on the FATF's draft virtual asset guidance. According to the submission, applying the Travel Rule to self-hosted wallets is incompatible with privacy rights. The FATF keeps widening payment transparency: in June 2026 it opened a consultation on new guidance for Recommendation 16, aiming for full implementation of the Travel Rule by 2030. Part 2 covers virtual assets in detail.

## About the data

- Grey list: 27 plenaries from February 2018 to October 2026, compiled from the FATF statements republished by Taiwan's Investigation Bureau, US FinCEN notices and New Zealand's Department of Internal Affairs, with each list checked against the one before
- Income groups: the World Bank's historical income classification (via Our World in Data), using the classification in force on each plenary date, counted by jurisdiction without weighting by population or economic size
- Effectiveness ratings: the FATF's consolidated assessment ratings (2013 methodology, updated 3 November 2026), using the latest report for each jurisdiction
- The data files are on [GitHub](https://github.com/anoni-net/www/tree/main/data/fatf)
