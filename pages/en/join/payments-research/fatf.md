Opening an account at a crypto exchange in Taiwan means uploading ID, sending virtual assets out means filling in the recipient's details, and opening a bank account means explaining where the money comes from. Most of these rules trace back to one international body, the Financial Action Task Force (FATF). The FATF cannot legislate and its recommendations are not a treaty, yet most countries write their anti-money laundering laws to follow them, and its public lists can move a country's capital flows. Any research on anonymous payments runs into FATF rules sooner or later.

## Origins and members

The FATF was set up at the 1989 G7 summit in Paris to develop measures against money laundering. Its secretariat sits within the OECD in Paris. It has 40 members: 38 jurisdictions plus two regional organisations, the European Commission and the Gulf Co-operation Council. Russia's membership has been suspended since 24 February 2023. Most members are G20 or European countries; the Asia-Pacific members are China, Hong Kong, India, Indonesia, Japan, Korea, Malaysia and Singapore.

With only 40 members, the FATF still sets standards for the whole world through nine FATF-style regional bodies (FSRBs). Together they form the Global Network, which covers more than 200 jurisdictions. Taiwan is not an FATF member. It takes part in the Asia/Pacific Group on Money Laundering (APG) as Chinese Taipei, and the APG carries out its mutual evaluations.

## The 40 Recommendations and how they grew

The core of the FATF is its 40 Recommendations. Countries write them into their own laws, and the FATF checks how well they have done so. The scope has widened over time:

- 1990: the first 40 Recommendations, aimed at money laundering
- 2001: after 9/11, special recommendations on terrorist financing
- 2012: the special recommendations folded into today's 40, with proliferation financing added
- 2018–2019: Recommendation 15 amended to cover virtual assets and VASPs
- 2023: Recommendation 8 amended in response to criticism that non-profits were being over-regulated

Each expansion brought more businesses and transactions under identity checks and reporting. Virtual assets are the latest, and the one most relevant to research on anonymous payments.

## How it works

### Mutual evaluations

Every country is evaluated periodically by a team of experts from other countries. The ratings come in two parts:

- **Technical compliance**: each of the 40 Recommendations is rated compliant, largely compliant, partially compliant or non-compliant, to show whether the laws are in place
- **Effectiveness**: each of 11 immediate outcomes is rated high, substantial, moderate or low, to show whether the system actually works

Depending on the results, a country is placed in regular or enhanced follow-up and reports its progress at set intervals.

### The grey list and the black list

The FATF plenary meets every February, June and October and decides two public lists. The grey list, formally "jurisdictions under increased monitoring", covers countries with strategic deficiencies that have committed to an action plan with the FATF; they leave the list after completing it and passing an on-site visit. The black list, formally "high-risk jurisdictions subject to a call for action", asks all countries to apply enhanced scrutiny to the jurisdictions on it, and countermeasures in the most serious cases. In October 2026 the black list was North Korea, Iran and Myanmar: countermeasures for North Korea and Iran, enhanced due diligence for Myanmar.

The grey list had 9 jurisdictions in February 2018, peaked at 26 in June 2023 and stood at 18 in October 2026. Reviews were paused in 2020 because of COVID-19, so the June list repeated February's. In October 2022 Myanmar moved from the grey list to the black list, the only escalation in the period.

## Where the leverage comes from

FATF recommendations carry no penalties. Their force comes from the way governments and the financial industry respond. Regulators pass the lists on to financial institutions: the US Treasury's Financial Crimes Enforcement Network (FinCEN) usually issues a notice after each plenary drawing attention to the listed jurisdictions. Taiwan's Investigation Bureau republishes the FATF statements on its website; in June 2025, for example, the Financial Supervisory Commission forwarded the Bureau's letter reminding financial institutions to apply enhanced scrutiny or proportionate countermeasures to black-listed jurisdictions. Banks also build the lists into their own risk assessments, and correspondent banks may scale back or end business with listed countries.

An IMF working paper from 2021 put a number on the combined effect. Looking at 89 emerging and developing economies from 2000 to 2017, it found that capital inflows fell by about 7.6% of GDP on average after grey-listing, with foreign direct investment, portfolio investment and other investment all declining.

<!-- fatf-imf -->

The authors note several limits. Grey-listing events are only about 0.7% of the sample, and the reasons for the drop (banks de-risking, investors using the list as a risk signal) are inferred rather than tested. The decline is large, but about the size of the 25th percentile of quarterly declines in emerging and developing economies during the global financial crisis, so not unprecedented.

## Where Taiwan stands

The APG adopted Taiwan's third-round mutual evaluation report in 2019, after an on-site visit in November 2018. Seven of the 11 effectiveness outcomes were rated substantial and four moderate (supervision, preventive measures, transparency of legal persons, and money laundering investigation and prosecution). On technical compliance, 10 Recommendations were compliant, 26 largely compliant and 4 partially compliant, with none non-compliant. According to Taiwan's Ministry of Justice, this placed Taiwan in regular follow-up, a level few APG members have reached.

Among the 199 jurisdictions with fourth-round evaluations, only six have more outcomes rated substantial or high than Taiwan, and all six are FATF members (France, the United States, the United Kingdom, Spain, Italy and Israel). Taiwan shares the top score among non-members with Kazakhstan, Jersey and Bermuda.

The reports came out between 2014 and 2024, under different standards and with different assessment teams, which limits cross-country comparison.

## How the FATF is judged

Both supporters and critics of the FATF can point to evidence.

### Does it work?

The FATF's own evaluations are sobering: 38.5% of all effectiveness ratings across 199 jurisdictions are low, and 41.7% of jurisdictions have no outcome rated substantial or better (the leftmost bar in the chart above).

Critics often cite Ronald Pol (2020). He estimates that about $3 trillion in criminal funds is generated each year and authorities confiscate about $3 billion, a success rate of about 0.1%. Compliance costs, using a LexisNexis estimate, come to about $304 billion, more than a hundred times what is recovered. Pol notes that the data are poorly validated and the methods inconsistent, so the findings cannot be definitive, but whether the real success rate is 0.1% or ten times that, the gap between policy intent and results is large.

Supporters stress the pressure the lists create. Julia Morse (2019) describes "market enforcement": the FATF has no sanctions of its own and relies on markets to punish countries that fall short, and listed countries see cross-border bank liabilities fall, giving governments a reason to reform. RUSI commentary credits the FATF with helping African countries break a cycle of repeated grey-listing; South Africa, Nigeria, Mozambique and Burkina Faso all left the list in October 2025. But Case-Ruchala and Nance (2020) re-ran Morse's analysis and found that her sample had only 10 countries; adding every country listed in the same period made the effect disappear. How much economic harm listing does remains contested.

### Is it fair?

Forty members set the rules by consensus, and the rules apply to more than 200 jurisdictions. Work by RUSI and U4 notes that African countries have almost no seat at the FATF table yet must carry out its decisions, and some commentators see the lists as a geopolitical tool.

Sorting grey-listed jurisdictions by World Bank income group tests the claim that the lists target developing countries.

<!-- fatf-share -->

Between 67% and 95% of grey-listed jurisdictions were not high-income, against 59% to 64% for the world as a whole, so the list does lean towards poorer countries. Every plenary also had one to six high-income economies on the list, such as Panama, the Cayman Islands, Malta, the UAE, Monaco and Bulgaria. The Basel Institute points out that the grey list names countries working with the FATF to fix deficiencies, not the world's highest-risk countries.

Evaluation results differ between members and non-members too. FATF members average 4.7 outcomes rated substantial or high; non-members average 1.4. Among high-income economies alone, it is 5.3 against 2.0.

<!-- fatf-groups -->

Critics can read the gap as peer review going easy on members; supporters can read it as members putting more resources into their systems. The data show a correlation and cannot tell the two apart. Regional members are not uniform either: the Gulf Co-operation Council is an FATF member and Saudi Arabia is a member in its own right, yet the UAE was grey-listed from March 2022 to February 2024, and Kuwait was added in February 2026 and remains on the list.

### Who bears the cost?

To reduce risk, banks may simply close accounts or refuse transfers, a practice known as de-risking. Non-profits, humanitarian remittances and people already on the edge of the banking system bear the brunt. Recommendation 8 used to treat the whole non-profit sector as vulnerable to terrorist financing, and RUSI points to Nicaragua, where the government used compliance with Recommendation 8 as cover to crack down on civil society.

The FATF launched a project on unintended consequences in 2021 and amended Recommendation 8 in November 2023, requiring countries to identify the non-profits actually at risk instead of applying measures to the whole sector. The Global NPO Coalition on FATF welcomed the change as a response to long-standing demands and said it would watch how it plays out in the new round of evaluations, an example of civil society changing FATF rules by taking part. RUSI commentary saw it as possibly cosmetic. The FATF's own stocktake notes that de-risking is hard to attribute directly to its standards, with supervisory pressure and reputational concerns also playing a part.

### What does it mean for privacy?

The FATF requires financial institutions to know their customers, keep records and report suspicious transactions, and wire transfers must carry information on the sender and the recipient (Recommendation 16, often called the Travel Rule). These requirements now reach virtual assets. Coin Center's 2021 comments on the FATF's draft virtual asset guidance argued that applying the Travel Rule to self-hosted wallets is incompatible with privacy rights. The FATF keeps widening payment transparency: in June 2026 it opened a consultation on new guidance for Recommendation 16, aiming for full implementation of the Travel Rule by 2030. ## About the data

- Grey list: 27 plenaries from February 2018 to October 2026, compiled from the FATF statements republished by Taiwan's Investigation Bureau, US FinCEN notices and New Zealand's Department of Internal Affairs, with each list checked against the one before
- Income groups: the World Bank's historical income classification (via Our World in Data), using the classification in force on each plenary date, counted by jurisdiction without weighting by population or economic size
- Effectiveness ratings: the FATF's consolidated assessment ratings (2013 methodology, updated 3 November 2026), using the latest report for each jurisdiction
- The data files are on [GitHub](https://github.com/anoni-net/www/tree/main/data/fatf)
