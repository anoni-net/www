title: One Year of Research on the Geedge Leak
description: After internal files from Geedge Networks and the MESA Lab leaked, researchers could for the first time compare the Great Firewall against the source code of commercial censorship equipment. This article reviews what two papers and civil society investigations confirmed over the year, what remains uncertain, and where news reporting and the papers' wording diverge.
date: 2026-10-30
---

For nearly three decades, researchers have had almost no option but to infer how the Great Firewall works from outside it, by sending crafted packets, recording which were blocked and how, and working back to the equipment behind them. When internal files from Geedge Networks leaked, researchers for the first time had the source code, build packages and tickets of a commercial censorship vendor closely tied to the Great Firewall. The authors of the USENIX Security 2026 paper state that this is the first time the source code of commercial DPI (deep packet inspection, which analyses the content of each connection to decide whether to let it through) has leaked. Researchers can now read the code first to find distinguishing traits, then go back to real networks to compare, instead of relying on black-box inference alone.

A year on, the matches found are narrower than some reporting has claimed. The measurements suggest that the leaked code, or a close derivative of it, is deployed on part of the Great Firewall's equipment. Other Great Firewall devices measured in the same way do not match it, no device matches the latest leaked version exactly, and for the other customer countries the results mostly stop at "potential" or "cannot confirm". At the same time, these conclusions rest on evidence from both the source code and measurement, which makes them more solid than earlier work based on remote inference alone. This article covers who did what research over the year, what has been established and what has not, for readers who build circumvention tools, do measurement research or follow supply chains. Technical detail stops at the level of mechanisms; readers who want more should go to the original papers.

## A Year of Research

The leaked files come from both Geedge and the MESA Lab. The MESA Lab (Massive and Effective Stream Analysis) belongs to the Institute of Information Engineering of the Chinese Academy of Sciences, and in GFW Report's description the two together are an important branch of the Great Firewall's R&D capacity. According to GFW Report, Geedge was founded in Hainan in 2018, with Fang Binxing, known as the "father of the Great Firewall", as its chief scientist. By the FOCI paper's count, the leak totals 572 GiB, comprising source code repositories, project management records, client deployment configurations and operational data, and more than 80% of it is an archive of an RPM package repository (RPM being a Linux software package format).

- **From 8 September 2025**: The Globe and Mail published its report, InterSecLab released "The Internet Coup" on 9 September, and Amnesty International's *Shadows of Control* and Justice For Myanmar's *Silk Road of Surveillance* came out the same month.
- **11 September 2025**: Enlace Hacktivista released all the files, and DDoSecrets also made them available for download.
- **12 September 2025**: GFW Report published an analysis of the leak, last updated on 29 December, with the note that "the source-code portion of the leaked files has not yet been carefully analyzed."
- **19 February 2026**: Researchers from the University of Massachusetts Amherst and GFW Report published the FOCI 2026 paper "Geedge Cases", comparing the domains in the leaked files with two widely used domain lists.
- **24 April 2026**: InterSecLab published "MADLink", the first instalment of a supply chain series, on the server and networking hardware Geedge uses, including hardware from ADLINK in Taiwan.
- **August 2026**: The authors published an explanatory post on their project site, geedge.report, on 5 August. The paper "Technical Analysis of the Geedge Networks Firewall Source Code Leak" was presented at USENIX Security 2026, held from 12 to 14 August, by authors from the University of Michigan, Paderborn University, the University of Massachusetts Amherst, the University of Colorado Boulder and GFW Report. Risky Bulletin reported on it on 21 August.

The news reports came out three days before the files were made public. In the introduction to the USENIX paper, the authors write that several news organisations and journalists "who were granted early access have worked together for the past year to analyze the documents from a non-technical perspective." According to the acknowledgements in ["The Internet Coup"](https://interseclab.org/research/the-internet-coup/), the Tor Project was also a partner in this joint investigation. The joint investigation worked from documents and tickets, establishing Geedge's customers, the countries where it deployed and the features of its products. Before the USENIX paper, the source code itself had been left largely unexplored. Academic research has followed a separate track: reading the source code, building the system and then running remote measurements.

The two tracks produce different kinds of evidence. From documents we can learn whom Geedge sold to and what its customers asked for, while measurement is used to judge whether a particular device behaves consistently with the code. Some reporting has applied conclusions of the first kind to the second (see the section on the gap between reporting and the paper's wording below for examples).

## TSG in the Source Code

### Build and Testing

The USENIX paper analyses Geedge's flagship product, the Tiangou Secure Gateway (TSG), which the authors describe as "a full-stack censorship platform designed to fully integrate into national telecommunications controls." The leak contains more than 100,000 internal documents and over 500 Git repositories alone, covering more than five years of commit history, with the newest files dating from November 2024. Analysis is hard because TSG's logic is spread across many repositories and the same component exists in several branches and versions, and the authors found at least two fully separate IP, UDP and TCP protocol stacks (the part of the code that implements basic protocols such as IP and TCP).

The research team read the code following the four stages of DPI: receiving traffic, reassembly and parsing, rule matching, and interfering with connections. For the build, the team started from the latest tagged release, mapped it across several repositories and package archives, and bypassed the licence check.

Once the build was complete, the team tested its handling of DNS, HTTP, TLS and QUIC (a protocol carried over UDP and used by HTTP/3) in an isolated test environment, successfully triggering injected forged responses, packet drops and deferred blocking, and used the rule engine to define a custom blocking target. The authors do not say whether all three application detection systems (see Third-Party Code below) were running in this build, so this article only goes as far as saying that the team built and ran TSG's latest tagged release and tested its blocking of several protocols locally. The authors deliberately withhold the full build procedure, for reasons covered in the research ethics section below.

### Core Components

TSG consists of several components that are developed and versioned separately. SAPP is the lowest-level packet processing platform, developed by the MESA Lab since 2005 through four major iterations, and written in C. SAPP assembles packets into TCP and UDP streams and hands them to plugins. It makes no blocking decisions itself and only provides the functions that blocking needs. Plugins come in three types: platform plugins provide shared functions, protocol plugins extract fields such as domain names from DNS queries and from TLS and QUIC handshakes, and business plugins use those fields to implement application logic such as blocking.

Stellar is a separate platform developed by Geedge in C++, with more complete connection state tracking than SAPP and richer ways for plugins to communicate. Stellar-on-SAPP is a SAPP platform plugin that converts SAPP streams into Stellar sessions for Stellar plugins to process, and it is explicitly identified in its README as a transition solution between the two platforms. In the leaked version, Stellar plugins run on SAPP through Stellar-on-SAPP. The standalone stellar repository appeared to be under active development at the time, was not referenced in any build repository, and had not been integrated into TSG.

Maat is the rule engine. Blocking rules are stored in Maat, and plugins match the fields they extract against those rules to get the action to take. Rules can match a wide range of fields, including transport and application protocols, the inner and outer addresses of tunnel encapsulation, source and destination IPs, ports, domain names and SNI (the site name sent in plaintext at the start of a TLS connection), ASN (autonomous system number) and country. The Firewall plugin is attached to both SAPP and Stellar, aggregates the fields extracted along each path, queries Maat and then enforces the block. Besides packet drops and RST (the TCP reset packet, which terminates the connection), blocking actions include throttling, HTTP and DNS redirects, mail and SIP blocking, tampering and deferred blocking.

The authors draw two points from the architecture, and both bear on every comparison that follows. Because components are developed and versioned separately, fingerprinting one plugin remotely (a fingerprint being a trait that identifies a system or version) does not mean another plugin is present in the same deployment, and reveals nothing about that plugin's version. Blocking decisions are made only after the results of several processing paths are aggregated, so a connection can be blocked on the result of any one of them.

### Third-Party Code

TSG has three application detection systems, which determine which app or protocol a flow belongs to, and two of them are built on third-party code. CBD (Context-Based Detector, formerly AppSketch) is TSG's own system and works from signatures loaded through Maat. Glimpse's protocol classification core is libprotoident, free software from the Libtrace team at the University of Waikato, and all the related code carries libprotoident's copyright notice. Glimpse's OpenVPN detection code, as the paper describes it, appears to be a modified copy of code from a 2022 version of nDPI (a GPL-licensed DPI library), with the main function renamed and the logic nearly identical. The third system, QDPI, is built on Qosmos ixEngine, a proprietary commercial DPI library; the authors base this on the code calling ixEngine functions and including its header file.

For libprotoident and nDPI, the paper's wording is that the code was copied and adapted. For Qosmos it says only that QDPI is built on it, and the authors make no judgement on whether Geedge had a licence. The team disclosed Geedge's use of this code to the libprotoident team and to Enea, the parent company of Qosmos. Both had responded by the time the paper was published and meetings had been scheduled, but what they said has not been made public. nDPI is not among the parties the paper lists as notified. In the authors' words, TSG's reliance on third-party libraries for two of its three detection systems "suggests that comprehensive protocol classification is costly to build from scratch, even for a well-resourced, state-linked vendor." Findings like these depend on reading the source code, since remote measurement can hardly tell whose code a detection system uses.

## Comparison with the Great Firewall

### Comparison Method

According to the USENIX paper, the Great Firewall was long thought to be highly centralised, but a growing body of evidence in recent years points to multiple DPI devices operating independently with different roles, and earlier measurement studies have named several of them. Devices doing DNS injection (a censor sending a forged DNS response before the real server can answer) are known as Injector 1, 2 and 3, names taken from "Triplet Censors" at FOCI 2020. Devices that send RSTs to terminate TCP connections are known as GFW I, II and III, alongside a separate regional censorship system in Henan province, names taken from "A Wall Behind A Wall" at IEEE S&P 2025.

From the source code, the team identified traits that can be measured remotely, such as idiosyncrasies in how protocols are parsed and a custom random number generator used to set packet fields. They then sent test packets between two hosts they own and compared whether censors in each location responded as TSG would. Measurements covered China's Great Firewall, Henan, Kazakhstan, Myanmar and Pakistan, with only DNS tested in Iran, across DNS, IP fragment reassembly, TCP, TLS and QUIC.

As the authors explain in the TLS section, a path may have more than one censorship device on it. A test packet that TSG lets through may still be blocked by another device, whereas a packet that TSG blocks is guaranteed to be blocked whenever it passes through TSG. Our inference is that when the blocking behaviour does not reveal its source, a measured "blocked" result could come from any device on the path and cannot on its own prove that TSG is there. Conversely, a result where TSG should block but the measured packet gets through rules out a TSG component with the same behaviour on that path. Modularity complicates things further: one deployment might run the same version of SAPP with a different version of the SSL plugin, so each comparison speaks to only one component.

### Matches

China's Injector 2 is the only injector that matches all three DNS traits: the flag value in forged responses, the use of compression pointers in those responses (the way a DNS packet refers back to a domain name already written), and the limit on compression pointers when parsing queries. The authors write that these results "strongly suggest that TSG provides the GFW Injector 2's DNS injection implementation."

When TSG forges RST packets, it fills several header fields using a custom random number generator. From the packets sent by GFW II and GFW III, a fixed seed value (the generator's starting value) can be worked back, and it is TSG's default. The authors estimate the probability of a constant seed occurring by chance at about 10⁻⁴⁴, let alone its matching TSG's default value, and describe the result as "strongly indicating usage of TSG code."

The same section lists where both deviate from TSG. The RSTs sent by GFW II and GFW III both use a fixed TTL (the count of how many more routers a packet may pass through), whereas TSG's TTL varies with internal state. In measurements from December 2023, GFW II's TTL was still consistent with TSG, which the authors take as suggesting that GFW II has slightly changed its implementation since then. Earlier research recorded GFW II sending several identical RSTs in a row; when the team configured TSG to send the same number of RSTs in a row, the IPID (the identification field of an IP packet) changed between packets.

IP fragment reassembly also produced a match: the maximum number of fragments GFW II reassembles at once is very close to TSG's. The authors are more cautious on this point, writing only that the result suggests "the two potentially share similar code."

The TLS tests altered each of the length fields in the ClientHello (the first packet of a TLS connection) to see whether a device still parsed out the domain and blocked it. No device behaved exactly like the latest leaked version. GFW II's behaviour is equivalent to an older version of TSG's SSL module, which, the authors write, "suggests usage of this older version in GFW II's SSL parsing." For QUIC, the Great Firewall and Kazakhstan blocked the same test packets as TSG, but both support different QUIC versions from TSG, so they cannot be matched fully. Kazakhstan's parsing fingerprint does fully match an older version of TSG's QUIC parsing code.

Section 6 of the paper sums up these results in one sentence, reporting high levels of correspondence with Injector 2, GFW II and GFW III, "suggesting that the leaked TSG code (or a close derivative) is indeed deployed on part of China's national firewall." The claim is framed as an inference by "suggesting", with "indeed" as the authors' emphasis. Most of the matches involve older module versions, and the geedge.report post likewise explains that the match changes over time and that the deployed code has drifted from the leaked version.

### Mismatches and Inconclusive Results

Injector 1 and Injector 3 do not match. In these measurements Injector 3 used different flags and no longer used compression pointers, unlike when it was first observed in 2020. GFW I, Henan, Myanmar, and the device in Pakistan that sends RSTs show no fixed seed. GFW III, Henan, Kazakhstan, and the device in Pakistan that sends RSTs do not reassemble IP fragments and simply forward them. The paper notes that whether to reassemble is an option in TSG's configuration file.

Myanmar's RST behaviour can be reproduced with TSG's settings, and its supported QUIC versions are exactly the same as TSG's, but its parsing fingerprint does not match, and the authors conclude that "we cannot confirm its use" in Myanmar. Some devices in Kazakhstan and Pakistan block by dropping packets, which makes it impossible to tell whether a test packet got through, and the authors write that both countries "support potential TSG deployments." Kazakhstan's censors do not send RSTs, so no RST comparison was done there. Even with a QUIC parsing result that fully matches an older version, the authors still do not describe Kazakhstan as a confirmed deployment. Iran's DNS flags differ from TSG's, and Ethiopia appears only in the leaked tickets, with no measurements.

The evidence that Geedge has customers in these countries comes from documents. The paper's background section cites civil society investigations listing deployments in Kazakhstan, Ethiopia, Pakistan and Myanmar, and the leaked Jira tickets mostly concern sites in Myanmar and Ethiopia. As far as the USENIX paper's measurements go, the only devices so far showing high levels of correspondence with TSG code are some of China's.

### The Gap between Reporting and the Paper's Wording

In Risky Bulletin's report, TSG is "one of the Great Firewall's three known traffic filtering capabilities", a phrase that appears nowhere in the paper. The "three" may come from earlier research's classification of Great Firewall devices. DNS injectors are divided into three, and the paper finds a match for one. RST injectors are also divided into GFW I, II and III, and the paper matches two. The researcher's post quoted in the same report also says that Geedge is only one of the vendors involved in the Great Firewall. The home page of geedge.report has the line "China's Great Firewall is running parts of TSG", which is likewise more assertive than the paper's inference.

The paper's results support a narrower claim: part of the Great Firewall's equipment uses TSG or a close version of it, the other devices measured do not match TSG, and there is currently no public evidence of who supplied them. Nor do the authors say that Geedge developed the Great Firewall; the words they use are "ties", "affiliated", and a "close collaborative relationship" with the MESA Lab. That is far narrower than claims equating Geedge with the Great Firewall, but it rests on evidence from both default values in the source code and remote measurement, which earlier research relying only on remote inference could not achieve.

## Circumvention Tool Signatures and Blocking

According to the USENIX paper, the leaked files show client-driven, iterative efforts to block specific circumvention tools, including Psiphon, Tor and popular VPNs such as ExpressVPN and NordVPN, with methods ranging from IP-based blocking to matching byte patterns. Signatures (rules describing the traffic traits of a given app) combine server IPs, domain names, TLS fingerprints, certificate fields, packet lengths and byte patterns. A Confluence document analysed in the paper records several updates to a signature bundle between March and November 2024, with signature risk levels between three and five, most of them five, while another test log aimed mainly at Chinese apps rates every signature one. The specific signature conditions are in the paper's tables, and this article does not repeat them.

Searching Jira for "vpn", the team found 700 tickets across 182 issues, covering reports of extracted server IPs, setting blocking priorities, repeated revisions after signatures stopped working, and over-blocking of other services. In one ticket, blocking failed once Signal's censorship circumvention option was turned on. The authors' summary is that circumvention tool signatures are tuned with customer involvement: customers can report failures, set priorities and flag over-blocking.

In the Psiphon records, obfuscation (techniques that make traffic look unlike any known protocol) raised the cost of detection. According to an internal document analysing the collateral damage of blocking Psiphon, Psiphon traffic lacks obvious signatures, so identification switched to server IPs, combined with a "whitelist protection mechanism" to avoid misidentification. The risk of over-blocking when blocking by IP and SNI also comes up in another ticket. In their conclusion, the authors set this alongside the other side of TSG, which "is capable of identifying circumvention tools at a relatively low cost," often matching very early in the connection.

The only material in the paper relating to TSG blocking Tor is the target list in the introduction and the signature risk level table, which includes an entry for Tor Browser. The paper does not describe the content of the Tor Browser signature and did not test Tor connections, so it reveals nothing about how TSG identifies Tor, still less that it can block Tor. InterSecLab's "The Internet Coup", which works from documents, reports that "Geedge Networks appears to lack any effective countermeasures against WebTunnel at the time of the leak" (WebTunnel is a Tor pluggable transport that disguises traffic as ordinary HTTPS web browsing), and that students at the MESA Lab had done preliminary research on ways to block it. According to the report, InterSecLab found no evidence of Geedge discovering or exploiting previously unknown vulnerabilities in Tor, and coordinated disclosure with the Tor Project.

The FOCI paper adds a piece from the domain side: several files in the leak specifically target VPNs and circumvention tools, including complete server lists for commercial VPNs and Psiphon's CDN domains. For developers of circumvention tools, server IPs and domains are themselves targets of collection and blocking, and traffic traits are only one of the methods.

As for code quality, the authors conclude that TSG's complexity and flexibility are "a double-edged sword": they make circumvention harder, but they also undermine code maintainability and correctness. The core components SAPP and its protocol plugins are written in memory-unsafe C, the system still relies on transitional solutions such as Stellar-on-SAPP, and code is copied from several third parties. The tickets include a case of an AppSketch database upgrade causing SAPP to restart, which the authors describe as part of a "patchwork" development process, and they see room for further study with fuzzing (feeding in large amounts of malformed input to find bugs) and symbolic execution (analysing a program's execution paths with symbolic rather than actual inputs). The paper reports no CVE numbers or specific exploitable vulnerabilities.

## Domain List Coverage

Censorship measurement starts by deciding which domains to test, and two lists are commonly used. Tranco is a ranking of popular sites by traffic, while the Citizen Lab test lists are maintained by hand by researchers and the community and organise sites likely to be blocked by content category. The FOCI paper extracted more than 14.36 million candidate domains from the leak, leaving 6,915,266 after resolution filtering. Vantage points in Guangzhou and Nanjing tested DNS injection, which is more reliable. Vantage points in Pakistan, Myanmar and Algeria tested whether TLS handshakes were cut off because of the SNI, which is less reliable. Each domain was tested 25 times so that intermittent blocking was also counted. Kazakhstan and Ethiopia are known customers, but the team could not obtain suitable vantage points there.

Of the leaked domains measured as blocked, 298,955 (93.7%) appear in neither Tranco nor the Citizen Lab lists. Conversely, the leak covers only 37.1% to 61.6% of the blocked Tranco domains. Each of the three lists has its own role. In the FOCI authors' words, "Tranco informs which globally popular sites are censored, CitizenLab monitors types of sensitive content over time, and the GNL [Geedge Networks leak] reveals which domains commercial vendors consider of interest."

Before reading these numbers, it helps to know where the domains come from. The FOCI authors do not treat a domain's appearance in the leak as meaning it is blocked. Explicit blocklists are sensitive customer data and rare in the leak; the domains mostly come from internal documents discussing customer environments and represent content "of interest" to Geedge or its customers. The largest source is the MESA Lab's SNI datasets, which the authors judge "appear to be gathered from network taps" (a network tap is a device that copies traffic on a line). The leak also contains copies of ranking lists such as Alexa and SecRank, used for MESA research. So the 93.7% describes a gap in the common lists. The leak offers the vendor's view, and which domains are actually blocked still has to be confirmed by measurement, the same approach the USENIX paper takes in reading the code first and then going back to the network to compare.

The community-maintained Taiwan section of the Citizen Lab test lists (`tw.csv` in `anoni-net/citizenlab-test-lists`) is likewise a hand-curated list, reflecting what its maintainers consider important. In light of FOCI's results, when measurement with a list finds no blocking, the conclusion covers only the sites on the list; domains outside it were never within the scope of observation. How to read a single measurement is covered in [OONI Blocking Verdicts](/projects/asn-coverage/ooni-blocking-determination/), and how many measurements each network has in [OONI Coverage](/projects/asn-coverage/).

## Supply Chain and Policy

InterSecLab's "MADLink" follows the leaked documents to the hardware supply chain. ADLINK, a listed Taiwanese company, shipped 1,708 CSA-7400 units (a high-density network platform the vendor markets for firewall and DPI use) to Geedge between 2019 and early 2020. According to the report, they were the hardware basis of the first-generation TSG platform, and in the leaked documents this configuration was deployed only in Kazakhstan. Geedge's own EtherFabric (a network packet broker that distributes traffic among multiple TSG nodes) is deployed in Myanmar, and in the leaked documents the vendor prefix of its management interface's MAC address is registered to ADLINK. On that basis InterSecLab judges that it contains ADLINK components, most likely a management compute board.

In its reply to InterSecLab, ADLINK confirmed the number of units shipped, and said that it had screened against export control lists before shipping, that Geedge was not on any restricted list, and that it would begin an internal audit. According to ADLINK, it supplies only general-purpose hardware platforms and was not involved in software integration or system design related to end use. InterSecLab's rebuttal is that the leaked documents contain records of TSG-ADC load balancing depending on ADLINK's PacketManager software, and that the CSA-7400 itself is marketed as DPI and firewall equipment. According to the Ministry of Economic Affairs, Taiwan's export control framework draws on international non-proliferation regimes and sanctions lists, and the ministry declined to comment on individual cases, citing legal restrictions.

The report argues that Geedge's current servers are mostly general-purpose x86 hardware that can still be obtained on the secondary market when regular procurement is blocked, and that purpose-built DPI and firewall equipment such as the CSA-7400 is where export controls can actually have an effect. We have made a [Traditional Chinese translation of the full report](https://anoni.net/docs/reports/interseclab-madlink/), and recorded [Taiwan's public response to the MADLink report](/updates/2026/05/madlink-editorial-2026-05/).

MADLink works from documents and the vendor's replies, and can be read against the USENIX paper's three measurements in Kazakhstan: QUIC parsing matching an older version, TLS inconclusive because of packet drops, and no RST comparison possible. There is a time gap between the two. The CSA-7400 is first-generation hardware shipped between 2019 and early 2020, years before the paper's measurements, and whether the devices measured are still that batch of hardware cannot currently be determined.

According to MADLink, the servers in Geedge's current hardware come from Nettrix, whose parent company Sugon is already under US sanctions. In a press release on 19 June 2024, Justice For Myanmar called on governments to sanction companies including Geedge Networks. As of October 2026, we had found no record of Geedge being placed on a sanctions list in OFAC notices, OpenSanctions or public reporting.

## Research Ethics and Data Handling

The ethical considerations section of the USENIX paper identifies five groups of stakeholders: Geedge employees and shareholders, third-party companies whose code is integrated in TSG, the countries deploying TSG, users under censorship in those countries, and the anti-censorship community. The leaked Git repositories contain developers' names and email addresses. The authors handle this by focusing on the systems and architecture, not reporting on individuals, and not including source material from the leak in their public repository.

Running a local copy of TSG carries its own risk, since it could help deploying countries refine their systems or make deployment easier for other censors. The authors therefore chose not to publish how to set up the firewall, and the published material is limited to what is useful for identifying deployments. The team also considered not running it locally at all. They concluded that with a working copy, the anti-censorship community can fingerprint deployments in different places, develop and test circumvention strategies against a real system, and generalise those strategies to other countries' firewalls.

Measurements were run only between two hosts the team owns, with no third-party server as a target, and when renting vantage points the team checked that the providers were not on the EU sanctions list. Neither the leaked source code nor the team's build is published, and the authors have committed to sharing the build with trusted researchers on request. What is public on Zenodo is the measurement results, anonymised packet captures and the measurement scripts. As a result of these trade-offs, other researchers can repeat the remote measurements with the published scripts, while the source code side of the comparison can only be repeated by researchers who are given access.

The FOCI authors take the position that the broad public interest of the information overrides potential intellectual property concerns, since the leaked data has already been made public by Enlace Hacktivista and DDoSecrets. The GFW Report analysis recommends analysing these files only in an isolated (virtual) machine without internet access.

Lantern's geedge.lantern.io takes a different approach. According to the page, it is a sibling to corpus.lantern.io, meant to tell circumvention tool builders exactly what they are up against, but the page does not say who is behind it, and its relationship to the Lantern circumvention tool cannot be confirmed from the page. The index does not rehost raw leak content, and each finding comes with a pointer to its source.

The findings in the index are extracted automatically by an LLM. According to the page, extraction is done by Sonnet-tier agents reading batches of extracted text, and the home page listed 880 findings when we read it. We sampled five, all marked at the highest confidence level. Three of them inferred that the system logs every connection from configuration field names alone, mostly citing test configuration files and giving no commit hash. Lantern's index can serve as a starting point for finding leads, but anything taken from it should first be checked against the original material, and it cannot be treated as evidence on its own.

In the net4people discussion thread, the only sourced follow-up research after October 2025 is the FOCI paper. The rest is mostly commenters' own comparisons and speculation, and some comments link to mirrors of the leaked internal systems, which this article does not cite.

This article holds to the same lines. It does not reproduce content from the leaked files, domain lists or file paths, does not give parameters, trait values or signature conditions that could be used to evade detection or improve blocking (the papers themselves publish some of these, and readers who want more can go to the originals), and does not mention the people who appear in the leaked data.

## Open Questions

Injector 1, Injector 3 and GFW I all fail to match TSG. The paper's results point only to part of the Great Firewall's equipment using TSG or similar code, and there is no public evidence yet on who developed the rest.

Kazakhstan, Pakistan, Myanmar and Ethiopia all have documentary evidence of a customer relationship, yet measurement stops at "potential" or "cannot confirm". The FOCI team could not obtain vantage points in Kazakhstan and Ethiopia, the USENIX paper had a vantage point in Kazakhstan but some results were inconclusive, and neither paper has measurements from Ethiopia. Deployments also keep changing. The leaked files stop at November 2024, and GFW II's TTL had changed between December 2023 and the paper's measurements, so future measurements will need to account for version differences.

The team's build loaded only three SAPP protocol plugins, DNS, SSL and QUIC, and the AppSketch database archives are all encrypted. The authors found no code converting the signature formats into Maat rules and only inferred such a conversion from similarities between the formats, and much of the source code has yet to be reviewed by anyone. The authors hope the paper will "serve as a guide for future research on the leak." The FOCI authors also list searching the leak for IP addresses, PDFs and better OCR (optical character recognition), and comparison with domain data from Common Crawl and ICANN as future work.

On the circumvention side, in the net4people thread tracking the leak (comments from October 2025 onwards), we saw no public statement from Tor, Psiphon or VPN providers on adjustments made in response to the leak. The Tor Project had reviewed the Tor section of "The Internet Coup", and the Psiphon team the Psiphon section. The leak has let researchers compare real censorship equipment against its source code; what comes next depends on whether vantage points can be extended to more customer countries, and on how tool developers use the results.
